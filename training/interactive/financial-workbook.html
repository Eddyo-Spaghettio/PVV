Welcome to Financial training

About 60 minutes in total

Funders don't fund good intentions. They fund clear needs, measurable outcomes, and budgets where every dollar has a job. PVV's financial team helps clients find funding and write grant materials, and this training walks you through that process with one practice client.

What you'll leave with A funder shortlist, a need statement, SMART outcomes, a checked line-item budget, and the opening of a letter of inquiry, all built for a practice client.

Module 1: Two doors of funding

About 8 minutes

Under PVV's constitution, the financial team assists nonprofits in writing grants. Before you touch a single application, learn the most common mistake new members make: confusing money for PVV with money for clients. They are different applications, different accounts, and different rules.

The PVV Treasurer manages the club's budget and makes sure operational grants like Haas Transportation Grants and Haas Center Event Co-Sponsorship are applied for. Amounts and deadlines change, so ask the Treasurer for current numbers. Your category chair directs the client grant work.

RememberPVV advises on Door 2. The client's executive director signs and submits. Our name is not on the application.

Module 2: Find funders that fit

About 10 minutes

The goal of prospecting is fit, not volume. Sending one generic proposal to a long list of funders doesn't work. A short list of funders who already care about your client's work and place does.

The fit checkDoes the funder support this location, this cause, and this size of request? Is the client eligible, for example as a 501(c)(3)? What is the deadline? Who have they funded before?

Module 3: Anatomy of a proposal

About 10 minutes

Most grant proposals want the same things in different words. Candid's introductory material lists the typical parts, and learning them lets you read any funder's guidelines quickly.

SectionWhat it answers
Executive summaryWho are you, what do you need, and why does it matter?
Statement of needWhat specific problem exists, and how big is it?
Project descriptionWhat will you do, and how?
Goals and outcomesWhat will change, and how will you measure it?
Organization informationWhy are you the right group to do this?
BudgetWhat does it cost, and where does the money come from?

Make outcomes measurable

VagueSMART
Help students80% of 30 students improve at least one grade level in math by June
Reduce hungerServe 200 families a month with weekly groceries for 12 months
Need statement formulaData: in [community], [X]% experience [problem]. Story: one person, with permission. Consequence: without help, [what happens]. Your angle: [org] is positioned to help because [evidence].

Module 4: Budgets funders trust

About 12 minutes

Many funders read the budget before the narrative, and they check that the two agree. A budget describes the project in numbers the same way the proposal describes it in words.

CategoryExamples
PersonnelPart-time coordinator, tutor stipends
Direct program costsFood, books, supplies for participants
TransportationVan rental, mileage
Administrative costsInsurance, office supplies. Many funders cap this, so check each funder's rules.
In-kind matchDonated goods and volunteer time, valued at a published rate
Golden ruleIf the narrative says "serve 45 students twice a week," the budget must show the staff hours and supplies for exactly that. Reviewers check.

For volunteer time, the standard is Independent Sector's annual estimate. For 2026 it is $36.14 per hour nationally, with a different figure for each state.

Module 5: Narrative and letter of inquiry

About 10 minutes

Many funders ask for a letter of inquiry (LOI) before a full proposal. It's a short letter that introduces your client and the request, and it decides whether you're invited to apply. Treat it as your best two minutes.

PartJob
OpeningWho the client is and what you are asking for, in one or two sentences
NeedThe problem, with one number
ProgramWhat the money will do
OutcomesOne or two measurable results
CloseThe total request, and a thank-you
Always follow the funder's guidelinesLength, format, and questions vary. Candid's examples of green flags include tailoring the proposal to the funder and answering each question clearly. Never send the same letter to every funder.

Module 6: Track, comply, and stay in scope

About 10 minutes

Grants run on deadlines and follow-up. A funding tracker keeps the client from missing either, and it's what a new liaison reads first when taking over.

Tracker columnExample
FunderA local community foundation
Ask and fit$10,000 for food purchasing
DeadlineA date, with a reminder two weeks earlier
StatusResearching, drafting, submitted, awarded, declined
Next step and ownerClient reviews draft by Friday

Stay in scope

  • PVV advises and drafts. The client decides, signs, and submits.
  • No legal or tax advice. If a client asks whether they qualify as a 501(c)(3), point them to their board or accountant.
  • Keep client financial information in the client's accounts, not your personal ones.
  • Beyond grants, PVV also helps clients navigate benefits and connect to available resources. Ask your chair before taking on anything new.
