Module 1: Your role and discovery

About 8 minutes

The most common technical mistake is building something the client didn't need, can't maintain, or already had. Discovery prevents that, and it comes before any building.

Five questions before any buildWhat problem are we solving? Who will use it? Who maintains it after PVV leaves? What's the timeline? What's the budget? ($0 is the default.)
NeedUse this (free)
Simple website or Reach PageGoogle Sites
Forms and sign-upsGoogle Forms or Tally
Data trackingGoogle Sheets, linked to Forms
DonationsThe client's existing donation tool, or a simple payment link

Our default is Google Sites + Google Forms + Google Sheets. The client probably already has Gmail, nothing needs coding, and you can hand it off in about 15 minutes.

Red flags"We need an app" usually means a form. "Make it like a big nonprofit's site" means a budget you don't have. "We need it Friday" when it's Wednesday means too much. No one on their team who can update content means it will go stale.

Module 2: Build the Reach Page

About 12 minutes

The Reach Page is the centerpiece of PVV's Bridge Kit: one mobile-friendly page where someone can volunteer, donate, or learn more in under two minutes. Most visitors will be on a phone, so design for that first.

The 5-second testA new visitor should understand what the organization does, who it helps, and what they can do, within five seconds of landing.

Build sequence

  • Intake: collect the logo, photos, and copy.
  • Draft the copy in a Google Doc, where the client edits, not in the site builder.
  • Build the pages, embed forms, and add alt text to every image.
  • Test on a phone. Click every link and submit every form.
  • Get client approval, publish, and write the handoff doc.

Module 3: Forms and surveys

About 10 minutes

A good form replaces a paper stack and feeds a spreadsheet directly. You're often collecting information from vulnerable people, so ask for less, not more.

  • Only ask what you need. Every field is a privacy risk and a reason to quit.
  • Use dropdowns and checkboxes instead of free text, so the data stays clean.
  • Add a consent checkbox, such as "I agree to be contacted."
  • Write a clear confirmation message that says what happens next.
  • For surveys, allow anonymous responses unless you truly need names.
  • Share the response sheet with named people only, never "anyone with the link."

Module 4: Data and spreadsheets

About 10 minutes

Most PVV data work follows one simple pipeline. Keep it simple and you can hand it off in minutes.

Keep data clean

  • One row per record, one value per cell. No merged cells.
  • Use dropdowns or data validation for categories, so "Mon," "monday," and "M" can't all appear.
  • Use one date format everywhere.
  • Leave the raw responses tab alone. Do your cleaning and counting in a separate tab.
A three-tab master sheetResponses: raw form data, never edited by hand. Working: the cleaned copy. Dashboard: the two or three numbers the client reports each month.

Module 5: Accessibility and mobile

About 10 minutes

Accessible design helps people with disabilities, and it also helps anyone on a small phone, in bright sun, or on a slow connection. For PVV clients, whose communities often rely on phones, it's not optional.

CheckHow
ContrastAt least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Check with a contrast checker.
ImagesAdd short, meaningful alt text. Decorative images get empty alt text.
FormsEvery field has a visible label.
LinksSay where they go. "Volunteer sign-up," not "click here."
MobileOpen the page on a real phone and try every button.

Module 6: Security, privacy, and handoff

About 10 minutes

You'll often handle information about vulnerable people. The client owns that data, and your job is to protect it and then hand everything back cleanly.

  • Keep client data in the client's accounts, not your personal one.
  • Share sheets with named people only, never "anyone with the link."
  • Never discuss individuals' personal information in PVV meetings.
  • Use long, unique passwords with a password manager, and turn on multifactor authentication.
  • Never put passwords, tokens, or API keys in a public GitHub repo. Keep them in environment variables.

The handoff document

Every project ends with a one-page guide for the client: how to check new form responses, how to edit the site's text, how to update the donate link, and who to contact if something breaks. Include an inventory of every account, who owns it, and who can access it.
